Yokohama wraps a heritage tall ship in projection mapping to anchor a winter light route

Edition 21·02 Aug 2026·3 min read

The story is not another illumination. It is a protected cultural landmark being used as a projection surface to pin down a city-scale route.

YORUNOYO 2026, one of Japan's largest illumination events, runs December 4 to 30, 2026 across Yokohama's central waterfront, with free admission and timed evening shows. The organisers list a record 50 participating facilities this year. The headline addition is the Nippon Maru, a sail training ship and nationally designated Important Cultural Property moored at Nippon Maru Memorial Park, which receives projection mapping for the first time: a 360-degree treatment that uses the entire hull as its stage and combines projection with mist and lasers. Elsewhere, Osanbashi Pier hosts one of Japan's largest interactive outdoor projection-mapping installations, and Yamashita Park carries a ten-metre light cube.

An anchor, not a scatter

The real signal is structural. Spreading a record 50 facilities across a waterfront risks becoming diffuse, a string of pretty but interchangeable installations. Mapping a single protected tall ship in full 360 degrees gives the whole route a hero surface, a fixed point people navigate toward and remember. The choice of the Nippon Maru matters: it is already a landmark on the harbour, so the projection borrows a shape visitors recognise rather than inventing one, and the cultural-property status forces a no-touch, light-and-media approach rather than any physical intervention.

Using mist and lasers alongside projection is the giveaway that this is built for volume. Mist extends an image off the hull into the air and reads from across the water, while the interactive installation at Osanbashi gives the crowd something to do rather than only watch. Together they turn a static illumination into a paced route with a clear centre of gravity.

What a producer can do now

For anyone planning a district-scale light programme, the lesson is to decide the anchor first. Choose one surface people already orient by, design its content as the fixed point of the whole experience, and let the smaller installations do the work of moving people toward and away from it. On a protected or heritage structure, plan for projection, haze and light as the only contact with the object, and resolve sightlines over water or open space early, because those are the views that photograph and travel. This is the planning behind a coherent activations kit, where a single hero moment and the lighter touchpoints around it are designed as one routed experience rather than a list of separate installs.

It also shows why a landmark projection belongs in an experiential toolkit and not just a lighting plan. The hull mapping is doing wayfinding, photography and dwell management at once, which is a content and show-control problem as much as a visual one.

Your takeaway

When a light programme has to cover a whole district, give it one hero surface to anchor on: pick a landmark people already navigate by, design its projection as the fixed point, and let the lighter installations route traffic toward and away from it.
