A Candy Land store built as a board game signals retail as a route to walk

Edition 21·02 Aug 2026·3 min read

The story is not the sweets. It is a store organised as a board-game route, with the checkout staged as the final scene rather than a counter.

Event Network is opening the first U.S. Candy Land immersive retail experience in New York this November, next to the New York Hilton Midtown on Sixth Avenue at 54th Street, under license from Hasbro, according to License Global. The format turns the store into the board game. Visitors move through themed zones as if they were game pieces, passing a Candied Apple Orchard, Gummy Mountains, Chocolate Caves, a Lollipop Forest and an Ice Cream Beach before arriving at a castle that doubles as the checkout. Along the way the space sells candy, drinks, toys, souvenirs, collectibles and apparel tied to each zone.

The real signal is the route, not the candy

It is easy to file this as another sugar-themed photo trap, and that reading misses the move. The decision that matters is structural: the store is organised as a path with a beginning, a sequence and an end, and the point of sale is the final scene rather than a counter bolted to one wall. Retail here is not a room you browse; it is a route you complete. The merchandise is distributed along that route so buying happens in character, inside the fiction, rather than as a transaction appended afterwards.

That inverts the usual order of experiential retail, where a brand builds a backdrop and hopes visitors buy on the way out. Mapping the game board onto the floor plan means the narrative path and the commercial path are the same path. Every zone is both a scene and a shelf, and the castle checkout turns the moment of payment into the end of the game rather than an interruption of it.

What a producer can do now

If you are briefing a branded space, start from the visitor's route before the set dressing. Decide the sequence of rooms, where the story turns, and where the transaction sits inside that sequence, then theme on top of that spine. A licensed world like Candy Land makes the route legible because the audience already knows the board, but the principle holds for any brand with a universe to draw on. Give people a path to walk, not a set to stand in, and let the purchase land as a beat in the story. The same logic underwrites a durable retail activation kit: sequence first, surfaces second.

Why it belongs in an experiential toolkit: a route-based store is reusable intelligence. Once you have designed one as a sequence of scenes with a built-in commercial endpoint, the structure travels to pop-ups, flagships and touring builds regardless of the IP sitting on top. The zones change; the grammar of path, scene and checkout does not. That portability is what separates a one-off spectacle from a method you can run again.

Your takeaway

Design the visitor's path first and the set dressing second. Put the purchase inside the story as a beat on the route, not a transaction bolted on at the exit.

Why it matters

When a store is laid out as a board game that visitors complete as game pieces, and the checkout is the final scene rather than a counter, retail stops being a room to browse and becomes a route to walk.
