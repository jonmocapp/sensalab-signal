Nike sequencing billboards to a marathon route signals media placed as choreography

Edition 21·02 Aug 2026·3 min read

The news is not that Nike bought out-of-home. It is that the placements were timed to the course, so the message changed as the runner moved.

Nike Running turned the 2026 Chicago Marathon route into a mile-by-mile out-of-home campaign, placed by billups, with trade coverage appearing around 12 to 15 October, according to Everything Experiential. Rather than a single hero site, the work ran as a route-wide series of billboards carrying short motivational lines that change from one spot to the next, so the messaging builds a sense of progression as runners advance. The creatives lean on bright yellow-green backgrounds and bold red type, with punchy copy: reported lines include 'Kick Asphalt,' 'Here Comes the Second Course,' and a paired 'The bad news: this is just the start' and 'The good news: this is just the start.' The copy carries no product claims and no athlete endorsements. Nike ran a comparable route-based effort for the 2025 race.

The real signal is media timed to the route

The surface story is that a large brand bought a lot of billboards. The real signal is that the placements were sequenced to the course. A board that reads 'Here Comes the Second Course' only works where it sits on the route, and the paired bad-news and good-news lines only land as a set encountered in order. That turns a media buy into choreography: the content is a function of where the runner is, not a fixed impression repeated everywhere.

The restraint is part of the mechanism. With no product shot and no endorsement to carry, each board does one job, which is to speak to a person at a specific, physically hard moment. The race supplies the context the copy would otherwise have to manufacture, which is why three or four words are enough.

What a producer can do now

Map the audience's path before writing a line. Any setting where people move along a known route, a queue, a concourse, a festival trail, a show floor, is a candidate for the same treatment: content that changes with position rather than repeating. Decide the beats first, where to reassure, where to push, where to pay off, and let the sequence do the work. The spot-by-spot change is the idea; a single clever board is not.

Why it belongs in an experiential toolkit

The same logic drives physical builds, not just printed media. Projection, LED and AR triggered by where someone is standing let an activation respond to a person's progress through a space the way these boards respond to a runner's progress through a city. Your takeaway

Map the audience's path before you write a word, then write to the moments along it. The change from one spot to the next, not a single hero board, is what turns paid media into part of the event.

Why it matters

When out-of-home is sequenced to a course, a placement stops being a static impression and becomes timed content, a format experiential teams can borrow for any route, queue or venue where an audience moves on a known path.
