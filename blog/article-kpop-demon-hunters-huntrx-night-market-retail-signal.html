Netflix and Walmart built a KPop Demon Hunters night market to sell a shelf

Edition 21·02 Aug 2026·3 min read

The news is not another fandom pop-up. It is a free two-night build in SoHo engineered to move a retail collection before the holidays.

Netflix and Walmart presented the HUNTR/X Night Market, a free fan activation tied to the film KPop Demon Hunters, over two nights on 10 and 11 October 2026 at 45 Grand in SoHo, New York, with the build produced by CNC Agency, according to BizBash. The floor was organised as a set of branded stations: a keychain-charm counter called Hunters' Charm Bar presented by Mattel, a neon-lit photo booth called Smile Studio presented by Colgate, and a ramyun counter called Shin Ramyun After Dark presented by Nongshim. Reported features included a wall of plush Derpy Tigers, arcade-style games and temporary bias tattoos. Walmart's role was explicit rather than scenic: the event promoted the official KPop Demon Hunters product collection sold at Walmart, and it was timed ahead of the holiday season.

The real signal is retail with a door, not a pop-up

The surface story is a fan pop-up for a hit title, which studios run all the time. The real signal is what the build is wired to do. Each station carries a partner that also sells product, Mattel, Colgate and Nongshim, and the whole floor points at a single collection a shopper can buy at Walmart. This is not an activation that happens to have merchandise nearby. It is a retail campaign that takes a physical, free-to-enter form for two nights, with the experience acting as the top of a funnel that ends on a shelf.

That reframes who the audience is and when the campaign is measured. A free two-night window in SoHo is a small room by attendance, but it is a content engine and a seeding moment before a holiday retail push. The partner-presented stations spread the cost and the reach across several brands at once, so the build does not have to justify itself on footfall alone. It earns out across Netflix's title, Walmart's shelf and three product partners' own channels.

What a producer can do now

If an activation is really a retail front end, design the exit with the same care as the entrance. Give every station a giftable object and a clear, short route to the collection it previews, so the walk-through resolves into purchase intent rather than a photo and a shrug. Treat partner-presented zones as a way to underwrite ambition: a single brand funds one room, but three partners fund a floor, and each gets a named, ownable moment. Our retail activation kit treats the shelf as the final scene of the experience, not a detail handled after the fact.

Why it belongs in an experiential toolkit

A build like this is worth studying because it is honest about its job. It uses fandom, free entry and giftable stations to earn attention, then spends that attention on a defined commercial outcome timed to a calendar. For a brand-experience lead, the transferable move is the structure: a short, high-density window, co-funded by partners, engineered so the memorable moments and the purchasable products are the same thing rather than two separate asks.

Your takeaway

If the activation exists to move product, design the exit as carefully as the entrance. Every station should hand the visitor a reason, and a route, to buy the collection that funds it.

Why it matters

A free, giftable fan build that routes straight to a retailer's shelf shows experiential working as the front end of a retail calendar, not a brand-awareness line item.
