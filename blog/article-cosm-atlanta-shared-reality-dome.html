Cosm's third dome opens in Atlanta, and immersive scale becomes a venue you book

Edition 21·02 Aug 2026·3 min read

The news is not a third immersive dome. It is that shared, screen-native spectacle now scales city by city as standing, bookable infrastructure.

Cosm opened its third Shared Reality venue in downtown Atlanta's Centennial Yards on June 10, 2026, with NBA Finals Game 4 as its first public event. According to the venue, the three-level, 70,000-square-foot building is built around an 87-foot-diameter dome carrying more than 12K of LED resolution. It sits beside State Farm Arena and Mercedes-Benz Stadium, following Cosm's earlier domes in Los Angeles and Dallas. The summer slate runs deep: 40 FIFA World Cup 2026 matches, the MLB Home Run Derby on July 13, the All-Star Game on July 14, and WWE SummerSlam.

The real signal is not another giant screen. It is a repeatable venue format, an immersive dome dropped into an existing stadium district and programmed like a channel rather than commissioned as a one-off. When the same 87-foot canvas opens in a third city in relatively short order, the cost of large-scale spectacle shifts from a bespoke, once-and-gone build toward standing infrastructure a producer can simply book by the night. That is a different economic model than the arena-scale spectacles most briefs have assumed.

A stage you can book, not build

This is where a producer's options actually change. Until recently, putting a client inside a shared, high-resolution dome meant financing a venue or renting a stadium and wrapping it in temporary LED. A permanent Cosm floor beside two major arenas means a brand moment can borrow that scale for a single evening, slotted between a Home Run Derby broadcast and a World Cup match, in front of an audience that already arrived for live sport. The room is the fixed asset; your content is the variable you control. It also compresses the timeline, since the hard part, the structure, the pixels, and the power, is already standing.

Why a dome belongs in the toolkit

Domes reward content built for immersion rather than framing. A flat wall asks people to look at a rectangle; a wraparound surface asks them to stand inside a place. That distinction matters most when the goal is a shared reaction, a full room responding to the same replay at the same instant, the way a crowd does during World Cup activations. For experiential teams, the move is to treat the dome as a medium with its own grammar, not a bigger television, and to design sightlines, motion, and pacing for a curved horizon from the first storyboard.

The format is the product

The Atlanta opening confirms a pattern the earlier domes only hinted at: shared, screen-native spectacle is becoming a distributed network rather than a single destination. For anyone mapping 2026 and 2027 activations, immersive scale is no longer a reason to build from scratch. It is a venue type to design for, one that will keep opening in more cities, on more nights, against a calendar already filling with tentpole sport. Producers who learn its language early will be the ones agencies call when the next dome lights up.
