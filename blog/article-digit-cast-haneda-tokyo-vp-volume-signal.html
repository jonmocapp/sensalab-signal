A 41-metre Tokyo LED volume signals virtual production built as standing infrastructure

Edition 21·02 Aug 2026·3 min read

The story is not another big wall. It is a studio wiring capture, volume and volumetric into one permanent pipeline a production can book whole.

Crescent has equipped its Digit-Cast Haneda Studio in Tokyo with a main LED wall measuring 41.5 metres wide by 10 metres high, two movable side walls of roughly six metres square, and an LED ceiling of about 23.5 by 15 metres divided into nine independently height-adjustable sections, according to trade coverage from Installation and others. The main wall uses next-generation AUO panels with a 1.95mm pixel pitch and 2,000 nits of brightness; the side walls run AUO P3.9 panels and the ceiling AUO P5.2. The full surface is driven by 27 Brompton 4K Tessera SX40 processors and 57 Tessera XD 10G data distribution units. The studio opened in October 2025 and reached full-scale operation at the start of 2026.

The real signal is the facility, not the wall

A wide wall and a bright pixel pitch are the easy numbers to quote, and they are not the point. The structural detail is that Crescent frames the volume inside a concept it calls Link-All, tying the LED stage to motion capture, volumetric video and photogrammetry across a dedicated stage building and a separate production building. The ceiling splitting into nine sections that move independently, and side walls that reposition, mean the same room reshapes to a different scene without a rebuild. That is a decision about throughput and standing capability, not about spectacle.

Read that way, the opening date matters more than the dimensions. A volume that has been running in full operation since early 2026, inside a facility that also captures performance and builds assets, is infrastructure a production schedules against, not a stage it borrows for a week. The investment sits in the integration, not the single hero surface.

What a producer can do now

When you brief a virtual production shoot, ask what surrounds the wall before you ask how big it is. A reconfigurable ceiling and movable walls let you reset between lighting states and camera setups in minutes, which is where shoot days are won or lost. Capture stages on the same site let you shoot a performance, rebuild it as a volumetric asset and place it back in the volume without moving the production elsewhere. Those adjacencies, not the headline width, set how much you can get through in a day.

Why it belongs in an experiential toolkit

The same logic carries straight into brand work. A reconfigurable volume that can hold a driveable car plate in the morning and a product world in the afternoon is the difference between a one-film set and a reusable stage a brand returns to. For live and hybrid activations, the capture-plus-volume pairing is what lets a real performer appear inside a rendered world and be reused afterwards. Your takeaway

If you are scoping virtual production, read the whole facility, not the wall size. A ceiling that splits into independently height-adjustable sections, movable side walls and capture stages on the same site decide how many setups you can run in a day far more than peak pixel pitch does.

Why it matters

When a studio builds a 41.5-metre LED wall, movable side walls and a reconfigurable ceiling into a permanent facility that also holds motion capture and volumetric capture, the virtual production volume stops being a rented stage and becomes standing infrastructure a production plans around.
